# Expose the generator, not the orbit

_(Substrate governance policy. Migrated from `memory/discipline/feedback_expose_generator_not_orbit.md`.)_

When Agda formalisation starts to look like:

  - 24 named Permutation record literals, or
  - a 24-case look-up table mapping (X, sel) → (v, s), or
  - any "n cases, mostly mechanical" enumeration with n large,

**this is a signal** that the wrong axis of the underlying structure
is being captured. The structure HAS a generator; we are formalising
the orbit instead.

**The discipline rule:**

1. **When you see a large enumeration coming, stop and ask: what is
   the generator?** S_4 is generated by V_4 ⋊ Stab(D); the 24
   permutations are the 4 × 6 orbit. Once you have `embed v · s`
   as primitives, the 24 are 2 lines of code, not 290.

2. **Python brute-force is a VALID prelude to Agda formalisation.**
   When the generator is hidden — e.g. you can't see the
   factorisation, or you suspect a structure exists but don't know
   its shape — write a Python script that enumerates the candidates
   and inspects the orbit. The Python output tells you whether the
   generator exists, what its order is, what the action looks like.
   Then the Agda exposes it.

3. **The generator must exist BEFORE the Agda or it must be built
   by the Agda.** If neither: the structure is genuinely 24
   independent entities (rare; usually the "case explosion" is a
   diagnostic for missing structure).

**Why:** The user, articulating this 2026-05-15:

> "B is something we can programmatically generate with loops in
>  another script to pull into agda — perhaps there's a way to
>  construct the maps via combinatorial functions, and let the
>  structure construct itself."

The Python-brute-force move and the expose-generator move are
two sides of the same discipline:

  - Python brute-force = DISCOVER the generator empirically when
    you can't see it from the catalog.
  - Expose-generator = let the Agda's composition produce the
    orbit; do not enumerate.

The two complement each other: Python finds the generator; Agda
exposes it as `f · g · h · ...` over the primitives it has, and
the orbit (= the "n cases") is the type-theoretic *consequence*
rather than the input.

**How to apply:**

- Before writing N case branches with N > ~8: stop and look for
  the generator. What pre-built primitives could compose to give
  N? Are there algebraic decompositions (semidirect products,
  fibrations, free products) that the catalog already records?
- If you can't see the generator: spin up a Python script.
  Enumerate the candidates. Look for regularities, periodicities,
  factorisations of the index space. Often the right decomposition
  becomes obvious from inspection.
- If you find the generator: rewrite the Agda to use it.
- If you don't (after 1-2 hours of Python): the structure may
  legitimately be enumeration-heavy. But this is rare.

**Concrete cost savings observed:**

- Slice 11b (Live ↔ Permutation): combinatorial route via
  `embed v · stab-from-selector sel` = 6 lines; explicit
  enumeration would have been ~290 lines. **~50× reduction**.
- Slice 2 (V_4 → S_4 embedding): 40 cases for `act-axis-∙` were
  irreducible because no generator was available at that scope —
  the V_4 group table IS the structure at that level. (Note when
  enumeration is structurally necessary versus when it's a sign
  of missed factorisation.)
